\section{Geometric machine learning}
\label{sec:geometric_machine_learning}
Geometric machine learning is a field of machine learning concerned with producing models that preserve the geometric structure of the space they are applied to.

This is achieved by carefully considering the space in question, the structure it has that we wish to preserve, and the symmetries that lead to this. 
This approach has two main benefits.

Firstly, it greatly reduces the problem space of possible solutions.
Typical deep learning models can learn to approximate any function, proved by ubiquitous \emphmarginnote{universal approximation theorem}s \cite{cybenko1989approximation,leshno1993multilayer,ismailov2023three,maiorov1999lower}.
However, the \emphmarginnote[2\baselineskip]{curse of dimensionality} \cite{bellman1957dynamic} means that as we expand the dimension of a problem space, the number of data samples needed to accurately estimate a function in reasonable classes, such as Lipschitz functions or Sobelov spaces, grows exponentially fast with the dimension of the space \cite{tsybakov2009nonparametric}.

This poses a problem for the generalisation of machine learning models in high dimension to data not previously seen.
\emphmarginnote{Function regularisation} schemes can help significantly with regularising the learnt function and improving generalisation, either explicitly via a penalty, e.g. \(L_2\) weight decay \cite{krogh1991simple}, or implicitly via the mechanics of stochastic gradient descent \cite{vardi2021implicit}, but this is not always enough.
Enforcing that models respect the structure of a given space greatly reduces the space of solutions, which in turn reduces the quantity of data needed to achieve a given accuracy.

For example, consider a simple \emphmarginnote{image recognition} task.
We aim to recognise if a cat or a dog is in the image.
If we apply a typical \emphmarginnote{multilayer perceptron} to this image, it will need to explicitly learn what a cat or dog is in every position in the image.
This is because it treats all pixels uniquely, preserving all the structure of the input.
On the other extreme, we can apply a network that is invariant to the position of pixels, treating their intensities as a histogram, such as a \emphmarginnote{transformer}.
This erases all spatial structure in the input.
This will fail to learn however as it cannot identify how pixels are arranged with respect to one another, preventing the recognition of relative multi-pixel structures such as ears and eyes.
In the middle of these is the standard \emphmarginnote{convolutional neural network} \cite{lecun1989handwritten}.
This respects that images can be translated up and down, left and right, and remain the same, but that if pixels are rearranged more than this, then it is a different image.
This strikes the right balance between preserving all structure of the input and preserving none.
As a result, a convolutional neural network takes significantly fewer parameters and computation to produce the same or better performance than a multilayer perceptron, and significantly outperforms a set-based model on this task.

The second result of this consideration is the application of machine learning models to \emphmarginnote{non-Euclidean data}.
Typical models make an implicit assumption that the data they are dealing with are vectors living in Euclidean space.
This is convenient as Euclidean vector spaces come with many mathematical tools.
There exists a significant number of problems that do not natively lie in Euclidean space, however.
By considering the inputs more carefully, we can expand the input spaces of models to a wide variety of \emphmarginnote{geometric spaces}, such as sets, graphs, grids, and manifolds.
This opens up the application of machine learning to a wide range of new problem domains, for example applications to social networks, molecular science, quantum physics, and climate sciences.

Overall, geometric machine learning aims to view the space that particular data lives on through its geometric structure and the symmetries they possess.
It allows us to unify common machine learning models such as convolutional neural networks, recurrent neural networks, graph neural networks, and many others into a single theory.
This also allows us to quickly design networks for new types of geometric spaces and transfer tools between them.

\subsection{Geometric priors}

Let us consider a set \(X\) on which we are going to place a machine learning model.
\(X\) may come with additional information.

Firstly it may come with a \emphmarginnote{geometric structure}, a set of relations telling us about how the points in the set are related to each other.
Secondly it may come with a \emphmarginnote{set of symmetries}, telling us how the elements can be permuted while preserving the geometric structure the space.

Two modelling scenarios arise in geometric deep learning.
Firstly, for geometric spaces \(X\) and \(Y\) each with their own geometric structure we may be interested in learning \emphmarginnote{functions between geometric spaces} of the form \(f: X \to Y\).
For example, consider a two dimension grid, \(X= \Z_n  \x \Z_n\).
The grid has a natural structure of points and their neighbours.
The structure is preserved if we translate the grid up/down or left/right, if we rotate the grid, or if we mirror the grid through an axis.
The grid structure therefore encodes translation, rotation and reflection symmetries, and tells us that models on a grid should preserve such symmetries.

Secondly, we may be interested in learning functions of signals on \(X\).
A \emphmarginnote{signal on a space\label{def:signal}} is a function that assigns a value in some space \(Z\) to every point in the space \(X\), a function \(f: X \to Z\).
Continuing the grid example, a signal \(f: \Z_n  \x \Z_n \to \sbr{0,1}^3\) assigning three intensity values to each point on the grid makes up an image.
We can denote the space of signals on \(X\) taking values in \(Z\) as \(\c{X}\del{X, Z}\).
A model that takes as input an image should respect the symmetries of the underlying grid.

\subsubsection{Symmetry groups}

Symmetries of spaces are encoded naturally in mathematical group.
The basic principle of a group of symmetries is that symmetries can be composed, i.e. that application of one symmetry after another is itself a symmetry, that symmetries can be undone, i.e. they are operations that preserve the information of the object, and that there is a symmetry that leaves the object unchanged.

Formally this can be summarised as
\begin{definition}[Group]
	A group \(\del{G, \cdot}\) is a set \(G\) along with an operation \(\cdot: G\x G \to G\) such that it has the following properties:
	\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1in]
		\item[\emph{Associativity}] For element \(f,g,h, \in G\), \( f\cdot \del{g \cdot h} = \del{f \cdot g} \cdot h\).
		\item[\emph{Identity}] There exists an element \(e \in G\) such that for every element \(g\in G\), \(e \cdot g = g \cdot e = g\). 
		\item[\emph{Invertibility}] There exists for every element \(g\in G\) and element \(g'\in G\) such that \(g \cdot g' = g' \cdot g = e\). This is typically denoted as \(G^{-1}\). 
		\item[\emph{Closure}] For every \(g, h\in G\), \(g \cdot h \in G\).
	\end{itemize}
\end{definition}
Groups can have significant extra structure in addition to this basic definition.
For example some group actions are commutative, \(g \cdot h = h \cdot g\), known as \emphmarginnote{Abelian group}, some are continuous functions, known as \emphmarginnote[\baselineskip]{topological groups}, and some have Riemannian manifold structure, known as \emphmarginnote[\baselineskip]{Lie groups}.
Often the group operation, like the multiplication symbol, is omitted, and instead operations are written as \(g\cdot h = gh\).

For example, the \emphmarginnote{cyclic group} of order \(n\) is defined by element \(\cbr{0, ..., n}\), has group composition \(\cdot: (g, h) \mapsto g + h \mod n\), and has identity element \(0\).
Taking a product of this group, \(C_n \x C_n = C_n^2\) defines a group of cycles in two directions.
The \emphmarginnote{dihedral group} \(D_n\) represents cycles with an additional flip operation. It has elements \((1, 1), ..., (1, n), (-1, 0), ..., (-1, n)\) where the first index indicates if there is a flip or not, and the second a cycle of order \(n\). 
It has group composition \(\cdot: (a, g), (b, h) \mapsto (a*b, g+h \mod n)\) and identity element \((1, 0)\).


\subsubsection{Group actions and representations}

Groups in the abstract are often not the objects we want to study.
Instead, we are interested in their action on some space.
The \emphmarginnote{(left) action of a group on a space} \(X\) is a function \(\triangleright: G \x X \to X\) that is compatible with the group operation, that is 
\[g \triangleright \del{h \triangleright x} = \del{g \cdot h} \triangleright x\]
for \(g, h \in G\), \(x \in X\).

To continue the grid example, the group \(C_n^2\) defines the translation symmetries of a two dimension grid of size \(n\), wrapping around at the edges.
The group \(D_4\) represents the rotation and reflection symmetry of the grid.
Their \emphmarginnote{semi-direct product}\footnote{A type of group product that means any element of the group can be decomposed as successive actions of one group and then the next} \(C_n^2 \rtimes D_4\) represents the combined translation, rotation, and reflection symmetries of the grid.

A \emphmarginnote{representation of a group\label{def:representation}} is a specific type of action of a group acting linearly on a vector space.
A (real) representation of a group is a map \(\rho: G \to \groupGL\), a map from the group into the space of invertible \(n\x n\) matrices.
This map must obey \(\rho(g\cdot h) = \rho(g) \rho(h)\) where the right-hand side is standard matrix multiplication.
Their action on \(\R^n\) and the group composition operations are given by the usual matrix multiplication.
There also exist complex representations, action on complex vector spaces.
Representations that map to orthogonal or unitary matrices are called orthogonal or unitary representations.
An introductory text to linear representations is \textcite{serreFiniteGroups}.

Representations are useful in building \emphmarginnote{actions on signals}.
Let us consider a signal on \(X\) taking values in \(\R^n\), \(f \in \c{X}(X, \R^n)\).
If we have a group action \(G\) on \(X\) and a representation of \(X\) acting on \(\R^n\), then an action of the group \(G\) on this signal can be defined by 
\[\del{g\triangleright_f f}(x) = \rho(g) f(g^{-1} \triangleright_X x)\]
for a representation of \(G\), \(\rho\).
This states that if we transform our signal by \(g\), then this involves mapping the underlying space \(X\) under the group action, and also mapping the observations under the same group action.
This whole operation can be seen as a single group action, and so again we can view the space of signals just as a geometric space in its own right.

To again continue the image grid example, the action of \(C_n^2\) will and translate an image, and the action of \(D_4\) will rotate and flip it.
The representation action on the colour channels of the image however is simply the identity action, leaving the channels unchanged.
This representation is the \emphmarginnote{trivial representation}, \(\rho(g) \to I\).

To see the action of a more complex representation, consider different data attached at each point on the grid, for example a vector representing the wind direction at each point.
In this case, we might expect that when we rotate the grid, the wind vector will rotate with it.
This is given by the \emphmarginnote{faithful representation}, a representation that is a bijection, of \(D_4\). If we let \(h = (a, r) \in D_4\), with \(a\) representing the flip component and \(r \in\sbr{0, 2\pi}\) the rotation component, then a faithful representation is given by

\[
	\rho(h) = \begin{bmatrix}
		\cos r &  -a \sin r \\ \sin r &  a \cos r

\]
If we place the action of this representation on the wind vectors attached to each point, then for \(g = (t, h) \in C_n^2 \rtimes D_4\), the action on the wind field is given by
\[(g\triangleright f)(x) = \rho(h) f(g^{-1} \triangleright x).\]

\subsubsection{Symmetric functions}

Two classes of function are of interest to us in geometric deep learning.
Consider a space \(X\) with a symmetry group \(G\), and a space \(Y\) with the same symmetry group.
\(X\) and \(Y\) could be underlying geometric spaces, or spaces of signals.

\emphmarginnote{Invariant functions} are functions \(f: X\to Y\) such that 
\[f(g \triangleright_X x) = f(x).\]
In the image analysis case an example of this is assigning a label to an image regarding its contents, for example "cat" or "dog".
The label does not change if the image is translated, rotated or reflected.

\emphmarginnote{Equivariant functions} are functions \(f: X\to Y\) such that
\[f(g\triangleright_X x) = g \triangleright_Y f(x).\]
An example of this might be locating where in the image a cat or a dog is.
If we translate or rotate the image, the location of the cat or dog will also translate or rotate.

The goal of many geometric deep learning architectures is to produce models that respect either the invariance or equivalence of the problem at hand.
In doing so, we automatically allow models to generalise to examples that they have seen before, but under a different group symmetry.

This is exactly how we can use geometric principles to reduce the size of the hypothesis space of functions that we try to learn from.
If we consider a hypothesis space of functions \(\c{H}\) between some spaces \(X\) and \(Y\) with a shared symmetry group \(G\), then the invariant and equivariant hypothesis spaces are 
\[\c{H}_{\text{invariant}} = \cbrcolon{f: X\to Y}{f(g\triangleright_X x) = f(x) \; \forall x\in X, \; g\in G} \subset \c{H}, \]
and 
\[\c{H}_{\text{equivariant}} = \cbrcolon{f: X\to Y}{f(g\triangleright_X x) = g \triangleright_Y f(x) \; \forall x\in X, \; g\in G} \subset \c{H}.\]
Depending on the original hypothesis space of functions and the group symmetry \(G\), this can be a significant reduction in the hypothesis space.
There exist various learning theory \cite{vapniknature,hastie2009elements} results that guarantee that under the assumption that the problem at hand does possess the specified symmetry, a model that respects this symmetry exhibit guaranteed better generalisation performance \cite{elesedy2021provably,elesedy2021provably2,lyle2020benefits,NEURIPS2022_257b9a6a}.

\subsection{Some types of geometric priors}
Here we briefly discuss a number of common geometric priors used in geometric deep learning.
We discuss the case of sets, graphs, grids, homogenous spaces and manifolds.

\subsubsection{Sets}

The simplest of geometric architectures concerns sets.
Typically, we only consider finite sets.
With no additional geometric structure, any network processing sets should not take into account the order in which elements are presented.
In building networks for sets then, we need architectures with \emphmarginnote{permutation equivariant} layers.

DeepSets \cite{DeepSets} and transformers \cite{vaswani2017attention} are the two most common architectures for learning on sets.

\subsubsection{Graphs}

Graphs bring one additional element over sets.
Graphs consist of a set of nodes \(\c{V}\) and a set of edges connecting some nodes together, \(\c{E}\).
Similar to sets, the goal is to create an architecture that is invariant to permutations of the inputs.
Each node and each edge may carry some information in the way of features.

Given the size of graphs typically processed, architectures aim not to use a global permutation equivariant function as it would be too expensive.
Instead, they use successive layers that apply local operations to nodes, their neighbours, and the edges connecting them.
These layers, combined with non-linearities and sometimes pooling operations, form the backbone of graph neural networks.

\emphmarginnote{Convolutional graph neural networks} apply a convolution to the features of the local neighbourhood to produce updated features.
Examples of this include \textcite{kipf2016semi, defferrard2016convolutional, wu2019simplifying}. \emphmarginnote{Attentive graph neural networks} apply attention between nodes and their neighbours' features. Examples of this include \textcite{velickovic2017graph,monti2017geometric,zhang2018gaan}. \emphmarginnote{Message-passing graph neural networks} construct messages between pairs of nodes from the features of each node, and aggregate them to produce updated features. Example of this include \textcite{gilmer2017neural, battaglia2018relational}.
These kinds of networks are useful for modelling structures social networks and other interactions.

In addition to basic graphs, a common extension is \emphmarginnote{geometric graphs}.
These are graphs that in addition to node features and edges include a spatial position at each node.
In addition to being equivariant to permutations of the nodes, we require that networks processing this kind of graph are also equivariant to global transforms of the locations of each node.
Examples of approaches to this type of structure include \textcite{satorras2021en,fuchs2020se}, taking message passing and attention-based approaches respectively.
Such models are useful for example in models of physics for modelling systems of particles interacting with one another.

\subsubsection{Grids}

Grids are a regular lattice of points in \(n\) dimensions, where each point is connected to its neighbours.
This can be considered a restriction of a graph which has a specific structure.

Most commonly in machine learning we deal with one dimension grids, representing \emphmarginnote{time series} of points, and two dimension grids representing \emphmarginnote[\baselineskip]{images}. 
Higher dimension grids are also possible to represent three dimension structures and more.

The primary natural group action on grids is translation.
In each dimension of the grid, we can translate the coordinates, moving each point to the point next to it.
This additional structure allows us to require \emphmarginnote{translation} equivariance of maps on grids, and this greatly reduces the space of linear operators to that of \emphmarginnote{discrete convolutions}.

In one dimension, recurrent neural networks exploit the grid structure to process each element in the grid in turn \cite{elman1990finding,jordan1997serial}, including extensions such as long-short term memory networks \cite{hochreiter1997long,rumelhart1986learning} and gated recurrent units \cite{cho2014learning}.

This structure is also exploited in convolutional neural networks \cite{fukushima1980neocognitron,waibel1989phoneme,lecun1989handwritten} and residual networks \cite{he2016deep}, along with a multitude of other works.

Beyond the translation symmetry of grids \cite{cohen2016group, cohen2016steerable} extend the capabilities of two dimension convolutional neural networks to be equivariant to 90\(^o\) rotations and reflections of the grid as well.

\subsection{Manifolds}
\label{sec:manifold_background}
\subsubsection{Topological manifolds}

We now give a more in-depth look at Riemannian manifolds and geometric machine learning on them, as they are the main setting of study in this thesis.
In this section we aim to give an introduction to the core concepts without delving into the details.
A more complete introduction can be found in \cref{chp:manifolds}.

A useful introductory series of textbooks on the subject are \textcite{lee2010introduction,lee2013smooth,lee2006riemannian} and the lecture notes \textcite{schuller2013lectures}. Other useful texts are \textcite{kolar2013natural, thorne2000gravitation}. \textcite{bronstein2021geometric} also contains a machine learning oriented introduction to Riemannian manifolds.

Like other geometric priors manifolds are sets equipped with extra geometric structure.
There are three main levels that we discuss.
Topological manifolds admit topological properties such as continuous functions.
Smooth manifolds in addition gain differentiable structure, allowing for the construction of differentiation and linear structures on the manifold.
Finally, the addition of a Riemannian metric adds usual geometric notation like distance, angles, and straight lines.


A \emphmarginnote{topological space} is a tuple \((X, \topo{X})\). 
This is a set \(X\) along with a \emph{topology} \(\topo{X}\), a collection of subsets of \(X\) closed under pairwise intersections and arbitrary unions, called \emphmarginnote{open sets}.
Topologies allow for the definition of notions of continuity, convergence of sequences and locality, amongst others.
Maps between topological spaces that preserve the topology are \emphmarginnote{homeomorphisms}.

A \(d\)-dimension \emphmarginnote{topological manifold} is a topological space with additional conditions.
It must \emph{locally} be homeomorphic to \(d\)-dimension Euclidean space, and it must be \emph{second-countable} and \emph{Haussdorff}. 
These properties make the topology of the manifold non-pathological.
Amongst other things, the Haussdorff property implies that the limits of sequences are unique, and the second countable property makes the notion of topological convergence and sequential convergence coincide.

On topological manifolds we may define \emphmarginnote{charts}.
Charts are tuples \((U, \phi)\), where \(U\) is an open set in \(\topo{X}\) and \(\phi: U \to \R^d\) is a map from \(U\) into an open subset of \(\R^n\).
A collection of charts such that the open sets cover the manifold is an \emphmarginnote{atlas} of \(X\), \(\atlas{X}\).
An atlas that contains all possible charts is a \emph{maximal atlas}.
\emphmarginnote{Transition functions} are defined on the region where for two charts \((U, \phi)\), \((V, \psi)\) overlap.
The transition function \(\phi \circ \psi^{-1}:\R^d \to \R^d\) is a map between open sets of Euclidean space.

Unlike Euclidean space, manifolds do not come with a canonical choice of coordinates.
We can induce a system of \emphmarginnote{local coordinates} through a chart, but since there are no canonical charts, there are no canonical coordinates on manifolds.
For this reason when working with manifolds we must define our various operations in a \emphmarginnote{coordinate-free} manner.
This is a large part of the difficulty presented when working with manifolds.

A \(d\)-dimension topological \emphmarginnote{manifold with boundary} is defined the same as a topological manifold, except that it may be locally homeomorphic to the Euclidean half-plane.
This notion encodes the idea that a manifold may not continue forever in a given direction.
The points on the manifold homeomorphic to the edge of the half-plane are known as the \emph{boundary} of the manifold, denoted \(\partial X\), and themselves form a manifold.

\subsubsection{Smooth manifolds}

A \emphmarginnote{smooth manifold} or \emph{differentiable manifold} \((X, \topo{X}, \atlas{X})\) is a topological manifold \((X, \topo{X})\) along with an atlas for which all the transition maps between charts are infinitely differentiable.
This allows us to define notions of differentiation on the manifold.
Maps between manifolds that preserve the smooth structure of the manifold are called \emphmarginnote{diffeomorphisms}.

If we can restrict the atlas of a smooth manifold to charts where the determinant of the Jacobian is positive for all pairs we call this an \emphmarginnote{orientable} manifold as it is allows for a consistent choice of handedness of local bases on the manifold.

On smooth manifolds we can define \emphmarginnote{smooth functions} \(f\in C^\infty(X)\) from the manifold to the real numbers as functions such that for any chart in the atlas, \((U, \phi)\), the composition function \(f \circ \phi^{-1}: \R^d \to \R\) is infinitely differentiable.

The \emphmarginnote{tangent space} of a point, \(p\), on a manifold, denoted \(T_p X\), can be thought of as a local linear approximation of the manifold, like placing a plane tangent to the surface of the manifold.
Formally it is made up of the vector space of \emph{directional derivatives} of smooth functions at the point on the manifold. 
Tangent spaces are isomorphic to \(\R^d\).
Unlike \(\R^d\) however, it does not come with a canonical choice of basis, again necessitating working in a coordinate-free manner.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \includegraphics{figures/diagrams/tangent_space_sphere.pdf}
    \caption{The tangent space of the point \(\icon{figures/diagrams/dot_red.pdf}\in \c{S}_2\) with example tangent vectors.}
\end{figure}

The \emphmarginnote{cotangent space} at a point on a manifold, \(T^*_pX\) is the vector space dual of the tangent space. 
By taking arbitrary tensor products of the tangent and cotangent spaces we can construct \emphmarginnote{tensor spaces} at each point on the manifold.
These are denoted \(T^p_q X\) for the tensor product of \(p\) copies of tangent space and \(q\) copies of the cotangent space.

The \emphmarginnote{differential} of a smooth function between manifolds \(F: X\to Y\) is a linear map \(F_*: T_pX \to T_{F(p)}Y\) that connects the tangent space \(T_pX\) to \(T_{F(p)}Y\). 
The \emphmarginnote{pushforward map} denoted \(F_*\), is another name for the differential.
The differential of a smooth function \(f\in C^\infty(X)\) is a notion similar to the gradient of function in standard multivariate calculus, except it maps a function to a cotangent vector, not a tangent vector as one might expect.

	\input{figures/tex/differential.tex}
	\caption{A smooth map between smooth manifolds \(F: X \to Y\) induces a natural map between the tangent spaces of \(p\) and \(F(p)\), the differential \(\d F_p\).}
\end{figure}


The union of all tangent spaces on the manifold, \(TX = \U_{p \in X} \cbrcolon{(p, v)}{v\in T_pX}\), combined with the projection operation \(\proj_X: (x, v) \mapsto x\), and the manifold itself gives rise to the \emphmarginnote{tangent bundle} of the manifold, \((TX, \proj_X, X)\).
This is itself a smooth manifold with a structure inherited from \(X\).
A \emphmarginnote{vector field} is a map \(\sigma: X \to TX\) such that the composition \(\proj_X \circ \sigma = \id_X\).
In other words, it assigns a tangent vector at a given point on the manifold to every point.
We denote the space of vector fields as \(\Gamma(TX)\).
While each tangent space on the manifold is isomorphic to \(\R^d\), a vector field cannot be expressed as a function \(\sigma: X \to \R^d\).
This is down to issues with choosing a globally smooth basis on \(\Gamma(TX)\).

The tangent bundle is an example of a \emphmarginnote{fibre bundle}.
This is a triple \((E, \proj_B, B)\), consisting of a \emph{total space} manifold \(E\), a \emph{base space} manifold \(B\) and a projection \(\proj_B: E\to B\).
The \emphmarginnote{fibre} of a point \(b\in B\) is defined as \(\proj_B^{-1} (b)\).
The fibres of all points in \(B\) are isomorphic to one another.
A vector field is an example of a \emphmarginnote{section} of a fibre bundle, a map that assigns every point in \(B\) a point in its respective fibre.
Stronger than this, it is an example of a \emphmarginnote{vector bundle}, a fibre bundle where the fibre is a vector space.

From the cotangent and general tensor spaces at a point on a manifold we can construct other vector bundles, namely the \emphmarginnote{cotangent bundle} and arbitrary \emphmarginnote{tensor bundles}.
Sections of these are known as \emphmarginnote[1\baselineskip]{covector fields} and \emphmarginnote[2\baselineskip]{\((p,q)\)-tensor fields} respectively.
The spaces of these fields are denoted \(\Gamma\del{T^*X}\) and \(\Gamma\del{T^p_q X}\) respectively.
The study of more advanced topics on manifolds involve the study of tensor fields.

As the vector space dual of tangent spaces, the elements of the cotangent space define linear maps over elements of the tangent space.
Covector fields similarly define linear maps over vector fields, where the linear map is defined pointwise on the manifold.
This operation is denoted by the \emphmarginnote{interior product}, \(\iprod: \Gamma(TX) \x \Gamma \del{T^*X} \to C^\infty(X)\).
Tensor fields are multilinear maps over combinations of vector and covector fields, and we can generalise the interior product to this case.

\emphmarginnote{Differential forms} are \((0,k)\)-tensor fields that at each point on the manifold are \emph{alternating tensors}, also known as \(k\)-forms.
As it turns out differential forms are particularly suitable to analysis.
The \emphmarginnote{exterior derivative} is a natural notion of differentiation that maps \(k\)-forms to \((k+1)\)-forms.
On smooth functions, which are \(0\)-forms, it agrees with the differential.

A nowhere vanishing \(d\)-form on a \(d\)-dimension manifold is known as a \emphmarginnote{volume form}.
The existence of these is guaranteed on an orientable manifolds.
These allow us to define a notion of the volume spanned by a set of vectors.
Using this we can define a consistent notion of \emphmarginnote{integration of smooth functions} on manifolds.
The choice of volume form is not unique on smooth manifolds however, and different choices of volume form lead to different integrals of the same smooth function.
Via the Riesz-Markov-Kakutani representation theorem we can connect a volume form to a measure-theoretic Radon measure on the manifold, a measure compatible with the topology of the manifold, known as the \emphmarginnote{volume measure}.
This generalises the \emphmarginnote{Lebesgue measure} to manifolds.

It should be noted it is possible to define a notation of integration on non-orientable manifolds also via \emphmarginnote{densities} on the manifold.

\subsubsection{Riemannian manifolds}
Riemannian manifolds add one additional point of structure over a smooth manifold.
They are a quadruple \((X, \topo{X}, \atlas{X}, g)\), containing a topology, smooth atlas and a \emph{Riemannian metric}.

Intuitively a \emphmarginnote{Riemannian metric} is a smooth choice of inner product on each tangent space.
More precisely is a smooth non-vanishing symmetric \((0,2)\)-tensor field.
These are guaranteed to exist for all smooth manifolds.
The metric also defines a \emphmarginnote{Riemannian norm} \(\norm{v}_g = \sqrt{g\del{v, v}}\) on the tangent space.

A choice of Riemannian metric allows us to canonicalise a series of choices we had to make on smooth manifolds.
Such choices as called \emphmarginnote{intrinsic choices}.
For example, it allows us to define a unique volume form on the manifold, \(\vol_g\), giving a unique notion of integration on Riemannian manifolds.

This unique choice of integration also lets us define an \emphmarginnote{inner product on smooth functions} on the manifold, \(\innerprod{f}{h}_g = \int_X f(p)h(p) \d\vol_g\) for \(f,h\in C^\infty(X)\).
Using this inner product we can define the Hilbert space \(L^2(X)\) of \emphmarginnote[\baselineskip]{\(L^2\)-integrable functions} on the manifold as the completion of \(C^\infty(X)\) under the inner product.
We can extend this to vector bundles on the manifold, for example for vector fields by \(\innerprod{V}{W} = \int_X g_p\del{V(p), W(p)} \d \vol_g\) for \(V, W \in \Gamma\del{TX}\).

By the Riesz-Fr\'echet representation theorem, which states that every dual vector can be expressed as an inner product with a vector, we can define a \emphmarginnote{tangent-cotangent isomorphism}.
We identify the covector \(\omega \in T_p^*X\) with the vector \(v\in T_pX\) if 
\[\omega(w) = g(v , w) \quad \forall \; w\in T_pX.\]
Using this we can define the \emphmarginnote{Riemannian gradient} of a smooth function \(f\in C^\infty (X)\) as the unique map \(\nabla_g: C^\infty(X) \to \Gamma(TX)\) such that 
\[\d f \iprod V = g\del{\nabla_g f, V} \quad\forall\; V\in \Gamma\del{TX}.\]
This is also denoted \(\grad_g\).
This definition of the gradient is now more similar to the usual multivariate calculus case as it results in a vector field, not a covector field.

We can also define the \emphmarginnote{divergence of a vector} on a Riemannian manifold via its volume form.
It is the unique map \(\dive_g: \Gamma\del{TX} \to C^\infty(X)\) such that
\[\d(V \iprod \vol_g) = \dive_g \vol_g V \quad \forall \; V \in \Gamma\del{TX}.\]
The divergence is also notated as \(\nabla^*_g\), as it is the dual of the Riemannian gradient in the sense that for \(V\in \Gamma\del{TX}\) and \(f\in C^\infty(X)\) \[\innerprod{\nabla_g f}{V}_g = \innerprod{f}{\nabla^*_g V}_g.\]
This definition of divergence lets us state a version of \emphmarginnote{Stoke's theorem} on a manifold with boundary.
\[\int_X \dive_g V \d\vol_g = \int_{\partial X} g(V, N) \d\vol_g,\]
where \(N\) is a vector field normal to the boundary.
If the manifold has no boundary then the right-hand side is zero.
This has the same intuitive meaning as in the Euclidean case.
It tells us that the integral of the divergence of a vector field inside a volume is equal to the flux passing through the boundary of the volume.

To define further geometric concepts on Riemannian manifolds we need another construction, a \emphmarginnote{connection}.
A connection is a map \(\nabla: \Gamma\del{TX} \x \Gamma\del{TX} \to \Gamma\del{TX}\) that is linear, distributive in the second argument and compatible with scaling. It defines a differentiation of one vector field with respect to another.
As such, the operation \(\nabla(V, W)\) is usually written \(\nabla_V W\).

On smooth manifolds we can choose many connections. 
With a metric however we can make a canonical choice, the \emphmarginnote{Levi-Civita connection}, that is compatible with the metric.
We will assume to always be using this connection.

A \emphmarginnote{curve on a manifold} is a map \(\gamma: I \to X\), where \(I\) is a connected subset of \(\R\).
The \emphmarginnote{velocity of a curve} is given by \(\gamma' = {\od{\gamma}{t}}\).
We say that a vector field is \emph{parallel} to a curve if \(\nabla_{\gamma'}V = 0\).

A \emphmarginnote{geodesic} generalises the notion of straight lines to manifolds.
We say that a curve is a geodesic if its velocity is parallel to the curve, that is \(\nabla_{\gamma'} \gamma' = 0\).
We define the \emphmarginnote{length of a curve} by the integral \(L(\gamma) = \int_I \norm{\gamma'(t)}_g \d t\).
A geodesic passing through two points on a manifold is provably the shortest path between those two points.
This allows us to define a \emphmarginnote{geodesics distance} on the manifold as \[\d_g(p, q) = \arg\min_{\gamma} L(\gamma), \; s.t. \; \gamma(0) = p, \; \gamma(1) = q, \; \gamma:\sbr{0, 1} \to \c{M}.\]

The \emphmarginnote{exponential map} at a point is a map \(\exp: TX \to X\) that maps \((p, v) \mapsto \gamma_{p,v}(1)\) where \(\gamma_{p,v}\) is the unique geodesic with \(\gamma(0) = p\) and \(\gamma'(0) = v\).
The inverse of the exponential map is the \emphmarginnote{logarithm map}, \(\log: X \x X \to TX\) that maps \((p, q) \mapsto v\) such that \(\exp(p, v) = q\).

In order to be able to compare tangent vectors in different tangent spaces we need a notion of \emphmarginnote{parallel transport}.
For a path \(\gamma\) and \((p, v) \in TX\), there exists a unique vector field \(V \in \Gamma\del{TX}\) such that \(\nabla_{\gamma'}V = 0\) and \(V(p) = v\).
We say that the parallel transport of \(v\) along \(\gamma\) is simply \(V(\gamma(t))\).
Note that parallel transport is entirely dependent on the path, and as such there is no canonical way to compare tangent vectors in different tangent spaces.
The norm of a vector is preserved by parallel transport.
If we parallel transport two tangent vectors along the same path, the Riemannian inner product of them is preserved along the path also.

On manifolds with boundary we can define a \emphmarginnote{boundary intersection\label{def:boundary_intersection}} operation that defines when a geodesic will hit the boundary of the manifold.
For a point \(p\in X\) and a unit tangent vector \(v\in T_pX\) we can define this as the function \(i: X\x TX \to \R^+\) such that 
\[
	i(p, \v{v}) = \arg\min_{t\in\R^+} \exp_p(t\v{v}) \in \partial X.
\]

Finally, can define a notion of \emphmarginnote{reflection of tangent vectors\label{def:manifold_reflection}} in a plane.
For a point \(p\in X\), a vector to be reflected \(v\in T_pX\), and a unit vector normal to the plane of reflection \(n\in T_pX\), we can define the reflection of \(v\) by the plane of \(n\) as
\[v - 2 g(v, n) n.\]
This is particularly useful for reflecting tangent vectors on the boundary of a manifold.

\subsubsection{The Laplace-Beltrami operator}

Fourier analysis and its analogues are key components in creating geometry-aware models on standard geometric structures such as graphs, grids, and homogenous spaces.
As such, we want to develop a similar analysis on Riemannian manifolds.

The \emphmarginnote{Laplace-Beltrami} operator is a key differential operator on Riemannian manifolds allowing this, and generalises the standard Euclidean Laplace operator.

It is defined as the operator
\[\Delta_g: C^\infty(X)\to C^\infty(X) \quad\quad\Delta_g = \nabla^*_g \nabla_g = \dive_g \grad_g.\]
This can also be defined via its expression in local coordinates, or via the trace of the second covariant derivative on the manifold.
This operator is coordinate-independent, and importantly commutes with isomorphisms of Riemannian manifolds, making it ideal for use in models respecting such isometries \cite[p. 46]{canzani2013analysis}.

The operator self-adjoint in the sense that for \(f\in C^\infty(X)\) \[\innerprod{\nabla_g f}{\nabla_g f}_g = \innerprod{\Delta_g f}{f}_g = \innerprod{f}{\Delta_g f}_g.\]
This can be extended further to \(L^2\)-integrable functions on the manifold, and also to \(L^2\)-integrable vector fields on the manifold where \(-\Delta_g\) is a self-adjoint unbounded positive-definite operator \cite[theorem 2.4]{strichartz1983analysis}.

Since these are Hilbert spaces, we can leverage spectral theory to decompose functions in the Hilbert space over eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of \(-\Delta_g\).
In particular for compact manifolds, the spectrum of \(-\Delta_g\) is countable.
\begin{theorem}[Strun-Liouville decomposition]
	\label{thm:strum-liouville}
	Let \((X, g)\) be a compact manifold.
	Then there exists an orthonormal basis of \(L^2(X)\), \(f_n\in L^2(X)\), \(n\in \Z^+\), such that
	\[-\Delta_g f_n = \lambda_n\]
	and that the basis is ordered in the sense that \(0 \leq \lambda_0 \leq \lambda_1 \leq \lambda_2 \leq ...\), and \(\lambda_n\to \infty\) as \(n\to \infty\).
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
	\textcite{chavel1984eigenvalues}
\end{proof}
This means we can write any function \(f\in L^2(X)\) on a compact manifold as the infinite sum
\(f = \sum_{i=1}^\infty \innerprod{f}{f_n}_g f_n.\)
This basis expansion can be truncated to a finite length
\(f_N \approx \sum_{i=1}^N \innerprod{f}{f_n}_g f_n.\)
The error of this basis truncation can be bounded.
\begin{theorem}[Bounded error of the Sturm-Liouville decomposition]
	Let \((X, g)\) be a compact Riemannian manifold, and \((f_n, \lambda_n)_{n=0}^\infty\) be the eigen-spectrum of \(-\Delta_g\).
	Let \(f \in L^2(X)\).
	Let \(f_N\) be the truncated expansion of \(f\).
	Then
	\[
		\norm{f - f_N}_g^2 \leq \frac{\norm{\nabla_g f}^2}{\lambda_{N+1}}.	
	\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
	\cite{aflalo2013spectral}
\end{proof}
It can also be shown that this approximation is optimal in the sense that no other basis will obtain a better error bound than the truncated Sturm-Liouville approximation \cite{aflalo2015optimality}.

\subsubsection{Riemannian isometries}

For two Riemannian manifolds \((X, g^x)\) and \((Y, g^y)\) and a map between them \(F:X\to Y\), we define the \emphmarginnote{pullback metric} \(F^*g_y\), a metric on \(X\) point wise via the pullback
\[F^*g^y_p (v, w) = g^y_{F(p)}(\d F_p(v), \d F_p(w))\quad \forall \; (p, v), (p, w) \in TX.\]
We call a map \(F\) such that \(g^x = F^*g^y\) a \emphmarginnote{Riemannian isometry}.

Such isometries and particularly the group of isometries of a manifold, denoted \(\isomg_g(X)\), onto itself, are key symmetries of study when producing geometry-aware deep learning models.

\subsubsection{The Nash embedding theorem}
\begin{figure}[h]
	\includegraphics[width=0.4\textwidth]{images/kernels_on_manifolds/illustration/s2_ev.pdf}
	\includegraphics[width=0.4\textwidth]{images/kernels_on_manifolds/illustration/s2_pr.pdf}
	\caption{A sphere isometrically embedded into Euclidean space. An ambient Euclidean vector field (left) projected onto the surface of the sphere (right).}
\end{figure}

\begin{theorem}[Nash embedding theorems]
	Given a \(d\)-dimension smooth Riemannian manifold \((X, g)\), there exists a smooth embedding \(F:X \to \R^n\) such that
	\[
		g = F^* g_{\R^n},	
	\]
	where \(g_{\R^n}\) is the standard Euclidean metric, for \(n \leq \frac{d(d+1)(3d+11)}{2}\).
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
	\cite{nash1956imbedding}
\end{proof}

The Nash embedding theorem is a cornerstone result of Riemannian geometry.
It allows us to take any manifold and embed it into Euclidean space while preserving the geometric properties of the manifold.

From a technical point of view embeddings are avoided as there is no canonical choice. 
However, it is useful in a few ways in machine learning.

Firstly for smoothly parametrising machine learning models, it provides a global way to smoothly specify the inputs to a function on a manifold via Euclidean coordinates, real numbers, and therefore a way to parametrise globally smooth functions on the manifold in a computer.

It also allows for an easy way to specify smooth vector fields on manifolds.
We cannot express a vector field on a manifold as a function \(V:X \to \R^d\), see \cref{def:projected_vector_field}.

Finally, it lets us compute differential operators on parametrised functions more easily using automatic differentiation.
For example, for a vector field on an isometrically embedded manifold the ambient Euclidean divergence coincides with the intrinsic divergence \cite{rozen2021moser}.

\subsection{Geometric deep learning on manifolds}

There are several distinct challenges when constructing deep learning models that act on manifolds.

Firstly, due to the lack of a canonical choice of coordinates for the manifold and for the tangent space, it is key to develop models that are independent of a specific parametrisation of the manifold and tangent space.

Secondly, we may want a method to respect the group of self-isomorphisms of a Riemannian manifold.
These symmetries are typically not global symmetries for most manifolds, and a large class of real-world manifolds do not have any symmetries.

Lastly, and most rarely, we may wish to develop methods that are invariant to \emph{diffeomorphisms} of manifolds, ignoring the metric structure and looking simply at the shape of the manifold.

Most of the architectures developed for manifolds deal with signals supported on the manifold, scalar functions on the manifold or sections of tensor bundles.
These approaches are based on manifold-aware convolutions.
\textcite{weiler2023equivariant} treats this topic in great detail, with a comprehensive development of these methods, and a deep coverage of the literature on this topic.

There is also some literature on methods for learning functions of the manifold itself.
Methods have been developed for functions of Lie groups \cite{huang2017deep}, Grassmann manifolds \cite{huang2018building,zhang2018grassmannian}, and general manifolds \cite{fang2023intrinsic}.
There is also significant literature for learning functions on meshes, discretised two dimension manifolds, for the purpose of representing textures \cite{koestler2022intrinsic, baatz2022nerf, chibane2020implicit, oechsle2019texture} and displacement maps \cite{yifan2022geometryconsistentneuralshaperepresentation}.


\section{Deep generative modelling}
\label{sec:deep_generative_modelling_background}
Deep generative modelling aims to combine the flexibility of deep learning with classical density estimation.
The aim is to overcome the rigidity of classical methods such as \emphmarginnote{kernel density estimation} \cite{rosenblatt1956remarks,parzen1962estimation}, and to overcome the curse of dimensionality present in these estimation problems.

The most common application of deep generative models include images \cite{saharia2022photorealistic,pmlr-v139-ramesh21a}, text \cite{team2023gemini,openai2024gpt4technicalreport}, and video \cite{videoworldsimulators2024}, and these application spaces are beginning to see widespread deployment in the real world.

In addition, there has been recently strong interest in applying deep generative modelling to scientific problems.
Applications have arisen in molecular physics \cite{zheng2023predictingequilibriumdistributionsmolecular,klein2024timewarp}, particle physics \cite{louppe2019adversarial,paganini2018accelerating}, quantum mechanics \cite{cheng2024highlyaccuraterealspaceelectron,PhysRevResearch.2.033429,doi:10.1126/science.adn0137}, statistical mechanics \cite{noe2019boltzmann}, protein modelling \cite{abramson2024accurate,krishna2024generalized}.

At a high level, the deep generative modelling task has access to a series of samples \(\cbr{x_i}_{i=1}^n,\; x_i \sim \pdata\) from some ground truth data distribution, to which we have no access.
We aim to learn a parametrised model, \(q_\theta\), which is close to \(\pdata\) in some sense.
This closeness is measured by a statistical divergence, \(\c{D}\), and the goal is therefore
\[\arg \min_{\theta \in \Theta} \c{D}\del{\pdata\from q_\theta }.\]
Since we do not have access to \(\pdata\), this is approximated by the \emphmarginnote{empirical distribution}
\[\pdata \approx {\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \delta_{x_i}},\]
replacing the data distribution by a mix of delta distributions of the data we have available.
Typical statistical divergences include the Kullback-Leibler divergence \cite{kullback1951information} (which can be seen as performing maximum likelihood inference), f-divergences \cite{renyi1961measures}, the Wasserstein distance \cite{kantorovich1960mathematical}, maximum mean
discrepancy \cite{gretton2012kernel} and integral probability metrics \cite{muller1997integral}.

Typically, we want to be able to generate new samples from this distribution and evaluate the likelihood of samples under our model.
In addition, we may want to perform \emphmarginnote{conditional generation} from our models.
If our data distribution is a joint distribution \(\pdata(x, y)\) where \(x\) is some high dimension observation such as an image, and \(y\) is a low dimension label or summary of \(x\), such as a text description, then we may want our models to be able to generate samples from a distribution \(q_\theta(x | Y=y)\), trying to match as closely as possible \(\pdata(x | Y=y)\).

There exist a wide variety of deep generative models.
Almost all of them exploit a strategy of taking random noise in some base space, \(z\sim p_{\text{prior}}\) and transforming it through some function to produce samples.

These can be categorised into a series of main methods.
\emphmarginnote{Generative adversarial networks} \cite{goodfellow2014generative} train two models, one generator \(G\) to create samples from noise, and one discriminator \(D\) which tries to distinguish between model samples and real data samples. 
The two models are jointly trained by minimising 
\[
	\min_G \max_D \E_{x\sim \pdata} \sbr{\log D(x)} + \E_{z\sim p_{\text{prior}}} \sbr{\log(1- D(G(z)))},
\]
where \(p_z\) is typically Gaussian noise.
These models are known to be unstable to train \cite{wiatrak2019stabilizing} however, and do not allow for likelihood evaluations.

\emphmarginnote{Variational autoencoders} \cite{kingma2013auto,rezende2014stochastic} apply a probabilistic perspective to autoencoders \cite{rumelhart1986learning}.
The data is assumed to be the marginal of some joint distribution \(p_{data}(x) \int p_{\text{joint}} (x, z) \d z\), where again for a sample \(z_i\) is a low dimension summary of \(x_i\).
A prior is placed over the latent space, \(\pprior(z)\).
The decoder, \(q_\theta(x \given z)\) attempts to model the conditional distribution \(p_{decoder}\del{x \given z}\), such that \(q_\theta(x \given z) p_{\text{prior}} (z) = p_{\text{joint}}(x, z)\).
The encoder, \(q_\phi(z \given x)\) aims to match the reverse distribution, \(p_{\text{encoder}}(z | x)\) so that \(q_\phi(z \given x) \pdata (x) = p_{\text{joint}}(x, z)\).
One observed drawback of variational autoencoders is that they tend to produce fuzzy or blurry samples, particularly in the image domain.

\emphmarginnote{Normalising flows} \cite{rezende15variational,tabak2010density, tabak2013family} exploit the fact that for an invertible function \(f\) and a distribution \(p_{\text{prior}} (z)\), the density of the pushforward of \(p_{\text{prior}} (z)\) through \(f\), \(\pmodel = f^* p_{\text{prior}}\), is given by 
\[ \log \pmodel(x) = \log p_{\text{prior}} (f^{-1}(x)) + \log \abs{\det\od{f^{-1}(x)}{x}}.\]
\(f\) is designed to be parametrisable, invertible, and have a fast to compute the log-det-Jacobian.
With this we can compute the likelihood of the data under this model quickly and therefore train it via maximum likelihood.
The requirement that \(f\) be invertible places significant difficulty on designing flexible \(f\).
To add complexity to the model, we can stack multiple transformations on top of one another.
However, this can be increasingly expensive and normalising flows can suffer from expressiveness issues and poor training dynamics.

\emphmarginnote{Continuous normalising flows} \cite{chen2018neural,grathwohl2019scalable} are an extension of normalising flows that replace the transformation \(f\) with a neural ordinary differential equation \cite{chen2018neural}, an ordinary differential equation with a vector field parametrised by a neural network.
This creates a model that continuously deforms noise into samples from the model.
They are also trained by maximum likelihood, and requires the computation of the change in likelihood induced by an ordinary differential equation.
This step can be expensive, and so continuous normalising flows can struggle to scale to higher dimensions.

\emphmarginnote{Diffusion models} or \emphmarginnote[\baselineskip]{score-based models} \cite{song2019generative,sohl2015deep, ho2020denoising} are a class of model that define a forward noising process that corrupts samples from \(\pdata\) under a specific nosing process.
These models then learn to reverse this noising process through a denoising process in order to generate samples.
We will explore these models in more detail in \cref{sec:score_based_models_background}.
Diffusion models have proven to be very effective.
They are fast to train, produce sharp, high quality samples, and place very few restrictions on the types of neural networks that can be used to produce them.
One drawback is that computing likelihoods under these models is either impossible, or very slow and non-exact.


\subsection{Score-Based Generative Modelling}
\label{sec:score_based_models_background}

In this section we give a presentation of the score-based generative modelling framework.

There are two perspectives on diffusion models; discrete-time diffusion models \parencite{sohl2015deep,ho2020denoising} and continuous-time diffusion models \parencite{song2020score}.
These are related, and the discrete-time models can be seen as a discretisation of the continuous-time framework.
As such, this presentation will focus only on the continuous-time version.
Throughout we will work with a state space of dimension \(d\), such that a data point is an element \(\v{X}\in \R^d\).

The core of diffusion models are \emphmarginnote{stochastic differential equations}.
These are random variables, denoted for example \(\del{\v{X}_t}_{t\in \sbr{0, T}}\), that place a distribution on time-indexed paths through a state space.
They are driven by an instantaneous change formula, in a common form given by
\[
	\d\v{X}_t = \v{b}(t, \v{X}_t) \d t + \v{\Sigma}(t, \v{X}_t) \d\v{B}_t.	
\]
\(\v{b}: \R\x\R^d \to \R^d\) is a \emphmarginnote{drift term}, like an ordinary differential equation, and \(\v{\Sigma}(t, \v{X}_t): \R\x\R^d \to \R^{d\x d}\) is a \emphmarginnote{diffusion term} controlling the instantaneous noise added into the stochastic differential equation, and \(\v{B}_t\) is a Brownian motion.
A technical introduction to measure theory, stochastic differential equations and various tools useful in the study of score-based models can be found in \cref{chp:sdes}.

A summary of the diffusion modelling strategy is:
\1 First we evolve \(\pdata\) under a stochastic differential equation, which we shall term the noising or \emphmarginnote{forward stochastic differential equation}.
We require that this stochastic differential equation converges to a known reference distribution, \(\pprior\) at large time scales.
This gives us a path between the data distribution and the reference distribution.
\2 Using a time reversal result, we can obtain a stochastic differential equation that has the same sample paths as our forward noising stochastic differential equation, but reversed in time.
Using this denoising or \emphmarginnote{reverse stochastic differential equation} we can create a map from \(\pprior\) to \(\pdata\), giving a generative model.
\3 All the terms in the reverse stochastic differential equation are known, except for the "score" term, \(\nabla \log p_t\), of the stochastic differential equation.
We therefore need a method to approximate or learn this term.
Fortunately, with the large \emphmarginnote{score matching} toolbox we have available to us there are several options for learning this score.
\4 Using the standard toolkit of \emphmarginnote{discrete sampling schemes} for stochastic differential equation we can sample the reverse stochastic differential equation in practise.
\5 Using the connection between a stochastic differential equation and an associated ordinary differential equations we can evaluate the \emphmarginnote{likelihood of a sample} under the model, using standard ordinary differential equation solving toolkits.
\0

See \cref{fig:diffusion_model_illustration} for an illustration of this summary.
Let us in detail look at each of these steps. 

\subsubsection{The forward noising stochastic differential equation}

The first stage of a diffusion model is to corrupt the data towards a known and tractable noise distribution, \(\pprior\).
The point of this step is to define a path between \(\pdata\) and \(\pprior\), with the intent later on of following this path in the reverse direction.

\emphhypermargin{Langevin dynamics}{sec:langevin_dynamics} are a type of stochastic differential equation.
If we have access to a density of the form \(p(x) \propto \exp(-U(x))\), then the stochastic differential equation \[\d \v{X}_t = \nabla U(\v{X}) \d t + \sqrt{2} \d \v{B}_t\] will in the limit of infinite time converge to \(p\), regardless of the distribution we initialise the stochastic differential equation with.

We can also rescale this stochastic differential equation by a time dependant noise factor, \(\beta({t})\), and obtain the same result, giving a stochastic differential equation of the form \[\d \v{X}_t = \beta(t) \nabla U(\v{X}) \d t + \sqrt{2 \beta(t)} \d \v{B}_t.\]
This time rescaling can be useful in diffusion models for optimising the efficiency of the discretised model rollouts and focusing the majority of the learning power of the score network on important regions of the stochastic differential equation.

Langevin dynamics gives us an option for our stochastic differential equation that converges to a known distribution.
Choosing the unit Gaussian as our reference distribution, \(p_T(\v{X}) \propto \exp\del{-\tfrac{1}{2} \v{X}^T \v{X}}\), we arrive at \(U(\v{X}) = -\v{X}\), and thus a forward noising stochastic differential equation of the form \[\d \Xf_t = -\Xf_t\d t + \sqrt{2} \d \v{B}_t .\]
This is the well known \emphmarginnote{Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process} which converges exponentially quickly towards the unit Gaussian.
In the literature this is typically known as the \emphmarginnote{variance preserving stochastic differential equation} or "VP-SDE".
A property that will be useful later is that the transition density in the forward direction, \(p_{s|t}\delvert{\v{X}_s}{\v{X}_t}, \; s > t\) has analytic density and sampling.
For a stochastic differential equation with noising schedule \(\beta(t)\) this is given by \[ p_{s|t}\delvert{\v{X}_s}{\v{X}_t} = \c{N}\delvert{\v{X}_s}{e^{-{\int_t^s \beta(\tau)\d\tau}} \v{X}_t, \del{1-e^{-2{\int_t^s \beta(\tau)\d\tau}}}\v{I}}\]

Another other common alternative is the \emphmarginnote{variance exploding stochastic differential equation} or "VE-SDE".
In this we simply apply Brownian motion as the noising stochastic differential equation,
\[\d\v{X}_t = \d\v{B}_t.\]
The transition density of this stochastic differential equation is given by \[\textstyle p_{s|t}\delvert{\v{X}_s}{\v{X}_t} = \c{N}\delvert{\v{X}_s}{\v{X}_t, {{\int_t^s} \beta(\tau)\d\tau}\v{I}}.\]
The variance of this will continue to grow with time and eventually the variance of this transition density will dominate the variance of \(\pdata\).
The distribution \[p_T(\v{X}_T) = \int p_{T|0}\delvert{\v{X}_T}{\v{X}_0} p(\v{X}_0) \d \v{X}_0\] will therefore be well approximated by \(\c{N}\delvert{\v{X}_T}{\v{0}, {{\int_t^s} \beta(\tau)\d\tau}\v{I}}\), and we can use this as the reference distribution.

A final alternative way to choose the forward stochastic differential equation is to start with the forward transition density and work backwards to the coefficients of the stochastic differential equation, which we will call the \emphmarginnote{\(\alpha-\sigma\) parametrisation}.
We restrict ourselves to stochastic differential equations with transition densities of the form
\[p_{t|0}(\v{X}_t | \v{X}_0) = \c{N}\del{\v{X}_t; \alpha_t \v{X}_0, \sigma^2_t}.\]
Let \(\alpha_t:\R\to \R\) be a differentiable mean function, with the restriction that \(\alpha_0=1\).
Let \(\sigma_t:\R\to\R\) be a differentiable noise function, with the restriction that \(\sigma_0=0\).
Then the stochastic differential equation that has this forward transition density is
\[\d\v{X}_t = \od{\log \alpha_t}{t}\d t +  \sbr{\od{\sigma_t^2}{t} - \od{\log \alpha_t}{t} \sigma_t^2}\d\m{B}_t.\]
If we pick \(\alpha_t\) and \(\sigma_t\) such that as \(t\to T\), \(\alpha_t\to 0\), then the process will converge to the distribution \(\c{N}\del{0, \sigma_T^2}\), giving us a reference distribution to work with.

The VP-SDE and VE-SDE fit into this framework, with \[\alpha_t &=  \exp\del{-\int_0^t \beta(\tau) \d\tau} \quad && \sigma_t = 1- \exp\del{-2\int_0^t \beta(\tau) \d\tau}\quad&&\text{VP-SDE}\\
\alpha_t &=  1 \quad && \sigma_t = \int_0^t \beta(\tau) \d\tau\quad&&\text{VE-SDE}.\]
It also allows us to easily explore other schedules such as the cosine schedule where we set \(\alpha_t = \sin \del{\frac{t\pi}{2T}}\), \(\sigma_t = \cos \del{\frac{t\pi}{2T}}\), or even linear schedules where we set \(\alpha_t = \frac{T-t}{T}\), \(\sigma_t = \frac{t}{T}\).

The choice of forward noising stochastic differential equation can have a significant impact on performance of a diffusion model. Interesting empirical studies can be seen for example in \textcite{karras2022Elucidating,karras2023analyzing,nichol2021improved,hoogeboom2023simple}.

\subsubsection{Applying a time reversal result}

The crucial step to be able to follow backwards the path defined by our forward noising stochastic differential equation is the application of a time reversal result.

We  prove a simple result showing that we can indeed create such a stochastic differential equation in the following, leveraging the \emph{Fokker-Plank equation}.

\begin{theorem}[The Fokker-Plank eqaution]
	\label{thm:fokker-plank_background}
	Let \(\del{\v{X}_t}_{t\geq 0}\) be stochastic differential equal, with initial distribution \(\v{X}_0  \sim p_0\) and equation \[\d \v{X}_t = \v{b}(t, \v{X}_t) \d t + \m{\Sigma}(t, \v{X}_t) \d\v{B}_t.\]
	Let \(p(t, x)\) denote the density of \(\v{X}_t\) at time \(t\).
	Then 
	\begin{align}
		\pd{p}{t} &= L^* p \nonumber \\
		p(0, x) &= p_0(x)
	\end{align}
	where \(L^* f = -\dive \del{\v{b}f} + \frac{1}{2} \m{\Sigma}\m{\Sigma}^T \Delta f\) and \(\Delta\) is the Laplace operator.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
	See \cref{thm:fokker-plank}.
\end{proof}

The crux of these results is that for a stochastic differential equation, we can find another stochastic differential equation with the same time-marginal distributions, but reversed in time.

\begin{theorem}[Fokker-Plank time reversal]
\label{thm:fokker-plank_time_reversal_background}
Let a stochastic differential equation, \(\del{\v{X}_t^\vsra{}}_{t\in\sbr{0, T}}\), called the forward noising stochastic differential equation, be given by
\[ \d\Xf_t = \v{b}(\Xf_t, t)\d t + \sigma(t)\d \v{B}_t, \quad \Xf_0 \sim \pdata\]
with time-marginal densities \(p_t^\vsra{}(\v{X})\).
Let another stochastic differential equation, \(\del{\v{X}_t^\vsla{}}_{t\in\sbr{0, T}}\), called the reverse-time stochastic differential equation, be given by
\[\d\Xb_t = \sbr{-\v{b}(\Xb_t, t) + \sigma(t)^2\nabla\log p_t(\Xb_t)}\d t + \sigma(t)\d \v{B}_t, \quad \Xb_0 \sim p_T^\vsra{},\] with time-marginal densities \(p_t^\vsla{}(\v{X})\).

Then the time-marginals are equal but reversed in time,  in the sense that \(p_t^\vsra{} = p_{T-t}^\vsla{} \;\forall\; t\in \sbr{0,T}\).
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The Fokker-Plank equation for the forward stochastic differential equation is given by 
\[\pd{p_t^\vsra{}(\v{X})}{t} = -\dive\del{{\v{b}(\v{X}, t)} p_t^\vsra{}(\v{X})} + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2(t)\Delta p_t^\vsra{}(\v{X})\]
Looking at \(\Delta p_t^\vsra{}(\v{X})\) we see that
\begin{align}
	\Delta p_t^\vsra{}(\v{X}) &= \dive \nabla p_t^\vsra{}(\v{X}) \\
	&= \dive \sbr{\frac{p_t^\vsra{}(\v{X})}{p_t^\vsra{}(\v{X})} \nabla p_t^\vsra{}(\v{X})} \\
	&= \dive \sbr{ p_t^\vsra{}(\v{X})\nabla \log p_t^\vsra{}(\v{X})} \\
	&= \dive\del{p_t^\vsra{}(\v{X})\nabla \log p_t^\vsra{}(\v{X})}
\end{align}
Adding and subtracting this identity  we get
\begin{align}
	&\pd{p_t^\vsra{}(\v{X})}{t}\\ &= -\dive\del{{\v{b}(\v{X}, t)} p_t^\vsra{}(\v{X})} + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2(t)\Delta p_t^\vsra{}(\v{X}) - \sigma(t)^2\dive\del{p_t^\vsra{}(\v{X})\nabla \log p_t^\vsra{}(\v{X})} \\
	&= -\dive\del{\sbr{\v{b}(\v{X}, t) - \sigma(t)^2\nabla {\log p_t^\vsra{}(\v{X})}} p_t^\vsra{}(\v{X})} - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2(t)\Delta p_t^\vsra{}(\v{X})
\end{align}
Taking the negative of this last expression we define
\[
	\pd{p_t^\vsla{}(\v{X})}{t} = \dive\del{-\sbr{- \v{b}(\v{X}, t) + \sigma(t)^2\nabla {\log p_t^\vsra{}(\v{X})}} p_t^\vsra{}(\v{X})} + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2(t)\Delta p_t^\vsra{}(\v{X})
\]
By definition then
\[
	\pd{p_t^\vsla{}(\v{X})}{t} = -\pd{p_t^\vsra{}(\v{X})}{t}.
\]
If we assume that for some \(t\in \sbr{0, t}\) \(p_t^\vsra{} = p_{T-t}^\vsla{}\), then by the \emphhyperref{Picard–Lindelöf theorem}{thm:picard-lindelof} we have that \(p_t^\vsra{} = p_{T-t}^\vsla{}\) for all \(t\in \sbr{0, T}\).
Since \cref{eq:rev_ode_form} is the form of the Fokker-Plank equation of the reverse stochastic differential equation specified, the claim follows.
\end{proof}
This proof is inspired by one found in \textcite[chapter 13]{nelson1967bm}.

This theorem states only that the time-marginals the of the forward and reverse distributions match.
For generative modelling this is all that matters as we only care that we can recover \(\pdata\).

More commonly cited in the score-based modelling literature are stronger results that guarantee the whole distribution on \emph{paths} of the forward and reverse stochastic differential equations match.
A discussion of commonly cited time reversal results can be found in \cref{sec:time-reversal}.
See \cref{fig:diffusion_model_illustration} for the difference between the matching of time marginals and the path distributions.

\subsubsection{Learning the score}

The next step in producing a diffusion model is learning the score term that appears in the reverse stochastic differential equation, \(\nabla_{\v{X}_t} \log p_t(\v{X}_t)\).
At first glance this may seem difficult, but a strong literature exists on learning this term, known as \emphmarginnote{score matching}.
In order to estimate this score function, we will use an approximation taking inputs \(\v{X}_t\) and \(t\).
We will denote this \(\scorenet{}: \R^d \times \sbr{0, T} \to \R^d\).
We will also use the notion \(\scorenett{} : \R^d \to \R^d\) to refer to this function partially evaluated at time \(t\), \(\scorenett{}(\cdot) = \scorenet(\cdot, t)\).
Typically, we will make this a neural network of some kind, and look to optimise it via gradient-based learning.

\textcite{hyvarinen05a} proves the following result:
\begin{theorem}[Implicit score matching (ISM)]
	\label{def:ism}
	Consider a density \(p_a(A)\) supported on \(\R^d\) relative to the Lebesgue measure.
	Then,
	\[\nabla_{A} \log p_a(A) = \argmin_{g\text{ measurable in }L^2} \E_{A}\sbr{ { \tfrac{1}{2} \norm{g\del{A}}^2 + \dive(g)(A)}}.\]
\end{theorem}
Using this we can learn the score of a distribution \(p_a\) if we just have access to good samples from \(p_a\) as we can approximate this expectation with Monte Carlo estimation.

We can apply this to our stochastic differential equation setting to say that the score at a given time is given by \[\nabla_{\v{X}_t} \log p_t(\v{X}_t) = \argmin_{\scorenett{}} \E_{\v{X}_t} \sbr{ \tfrac{1}{2} \norm{\scorenett{}(\v{X}_t)}^2 + \dive(\scorenett{})(\v{X}_t)}.\]
We denote then this loss function as \(\ismlosst{}\).

In order to draw samples from \(p_t(\v{X}_t)\) we can utilise the fact that \[p_t(\v{X}_t) = \int p_{t|0}(\v{X}_t) p_0(\v{X}_0) \d \v{X}_0.\]
We can sample then from this joint density as we have samples from \(p_0 = \pdata\), and we have access to sampling from the transition density \( p_{t|0}\) from the forward noising stochastic differential equation.
We then discard the samples from \(p_0\) to obtain samples from \(p_t\).

A limitation of this objective is that it requires computation of the divergence of the score.
While this is achievable using automatic differentiation packages, it is expensive in high dimension, scaling in cost with \(\c{O}(d^2)\).

It is possible to apply to this the Hutchinson trace trick estimator \parencite{hutchinson1989stochastic} to stochastically approximate the divergence to get a loss function that scales with \(\c{O}(d)\) at the cost of higher variance, as detailed in \textcite{song2020sliced}, where it is called \emphmarginnote{sliced score matching (SSM)}.
We denote this loss function \(\ssmlosst{}\).
In practise this additional variance does not seem to be too important, as the variance of the Monte Carlo estimation of the integral over \(p_t\) is larger.

\emphmarginnote{Denoising score matching (DSM) \label{def:dsm}} \parencite{vincent2011connection} is an alternative approach to implicit score matching that typically has lower variance.
However, applying this objective requires additional tractability on the forward noising process.
Recall that the definition of the conditional distribution states that for \(L^2\)-integrable random variables \(A\) and \(B\), the conditional expectation map
\[g^*: b\mapsto\E_{A}\sbrvert{A}{B=b}\]
is given by the minimiser
\[g^* = \argmin_{g\text{ measurable in }L^2} \E_{A, B}\sbr{\norm{A - g(B)}^2}\] where \(\E_{A}\sbrvert{A}{B=b}\) is the conditional expectation of \(A\) given a value of \(B\).
We apply this by putting \(A = \nabla \log p_{c|d}\delvert{C}{D}\) and \(B = C\) for new random variables \(C\) and \(D\), assuming they have densities relative to the Lebesgue measure, and so \[g(C) = \E_{D}\sbrvert{ \nabla \log p_{c|d}\delvert{D}{C}}{C}.\]
We can then apply the Tweedie identity.
\begin{lemma}[The Tweedie identity]\label{lem:tweedie}
	\[\E_{D}\sbrvert{\nabla_{C} \log p_{c|d}\delvert{C}{D}}{C} = \nabla_{C} \log p_c(C)\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
    \[
		\E_D&\sbrvert{\nabla_{C} \log p_{c|d}\delvert{C}{D}}{C} \nonumber\\
		&= \int_{\R^d} \nabla_{C} \log p_{c|d}\delvert{C}{D} p_{d|c}(D | C) \d D \nonumber\marginnote{By definition}\\
		&= \int_{R^d} \frac{\nabla_{C} p_{c|d}\delvert{C}{D}}{p_{c|d}\delvert{C}{D}} \frac{p_{c|d}\delvert{C}{D}p_d(D)}{p_t(C)} \d D\nonumber\marginnote{By the gradient of log and Bayess rule}\\
		&= \frac{1}{p_t\del{C}} \int_{\R^d} p_d\del{D} \nabla_{C} p_{c|d}\delvert{C}{D} \d D\nonumber\marginnote{Cancel terms and remove non-integrands}\\
		&= \frac{1}{p_t\del{C}} \nabla_{C}\int_{\R^d} p_d\del{D}  p_{c|d}\delvert{C}{D} \d D\nonumber\marginnote{By linearity of integrals}\\
		&= \frac{\nabla_{C}p_{t}\del{C}}{p_c\del{C}} = \nabla_{C}\log p_c\del{C}\nonumber\marginnote{By the gradient of log}
 	\]
\end{proof}
Combining this we see that \[ \argmin_{g\text{ measurable in }L^2} \E_{C,D}\sbr{\norm{\nabla_C \log p_{c|d}\delvert{C}{D} - g(C)}^2} &= \E_{D}\sbrvert{\nabla_{C} \log p_{c|d}\delvert{C}{D}}{C}\nonumber \\ &= \nabla_{C} \log p_c(C),\] and therefore we can learn the score for \(\nabla_C \log p_c(C)\) if we have access to \1 samples from the joint distribution \(p_{c,d}(C, D)\) and \2 access to the analytic conditional score \(\nabla_C \log p_{c|d} \delvert{C}{D}\).\0

To apply this to our stochastic differential equation setting, we pick \(C = \v{X}_t\) and \(D=\v{X}_s\), with \(s < t\). We have access to samples from \(p_{t,s}\del{\v{X}_t, \v{X}_s}\) by using the fact that 
\[
	p_{t,s}\del{\v{X}_t, \v{X}_s} &= \int_{\R^d} p_{t|s, 0}\delvert{\v{X}_t}{\v{X}_s, \v{X}_0} p_{s|0} \delvert{\v{X}_s}{\v{X}_0} p_0\del{\v{X}_0} \d \v{X}_0 \\
	&= \int_{\R^d} p_{t|s}\delvert{\v{X}_t}{\v{X}_s} p_{s|0} \delvert{\v{X}_s}{\v{X}_0} p_0\del{\v{X}_0} \d \v{X}_0
\] where the second line follows as stochastic differential equation transitions satisfy the Markov property. We have access to samples from \(p_0\) from our data, and the stochastic differential equation tells us how to sample from the transitions.
We also need access to the analytic form of \(\nabla_{\v{X}_t} \log p_{t|s} \delvert{\v{X}_t}{\v{X}_s}\).
For most stochastic differential equations used in the literature, such as the variance exploding and preserving, this is available.
If it is not available, often good approximations are available over for short time steps between \(s\) and \(t\).

The most common form of this loss in the literature is to set \(s=0\). 
This simplifies the sampling as then we only need to sample a single transition.
This is the denoising score matching described in \textcite{song2020score}.
We describe the more general scheme here as it is useful when we only have access to an approximation of \(p_{t|s}\) over short times.

We shall label this loss \(\gdsmlosst{}\) in the general case and \(\dsmlosst{}\) when using \(s=0\).

To summarise:

The objectives detailed match the score at given time \(t\) only. 
In order to match the score at all times, we integrate this objective over all times \(t \in \sbr{0, T}\).
\[\ell(\scorenet{}) = \int_{\sbr{0, T}} \lambda(t) \ell_t(\scorenett{}) \d t.\]
Notice there is a weighting function applied over all \(t\).
While this weighting does not affect the minima of the objective, it does rebalance where a network will prioritise learning the score most.
Many choices for \(\lambda(t)\) exist and can be tuned to optimise different objectives.

A common feature of all of these objectives is that they are
\1 stable regression objectives and
\2 require evaluation of the score network at a given time \(t\) only.
\0
Compare this to the objectives of normalising flow models, VAE models, or GANs.
These objective functions require us to run a full generation of samples from the model in order to evaluate the objective.
In contrast, the score matching objectives only need us to evaluate the generative model at one point in the generative process.

Additionally, the score matching objectives at a given time are stable with respect to other times.
That is, the minima at a given time will not change as we optimise the score function at other times.
This is in comparison to most objectives of other deep generative models, where changing the weights at one point in the network changes the optimum weights at other points in the network are.
This is exacerbated in GANs, as changing the discriminator function changes the objective the generator function is trying to learn, leading to well known instabilities.
This property ensured by the fact that in diffusion models we specify the path we wish the model to take from noise to data.
In other models it is left to the model to find this path.

There is one drawback to score-matching objectives however, and that is the Monte Carlo sampling required to evaluate them makes them particularly noisy.
This can be mitigated however by taking an average of the weights throughout training.
Typically, exponential moving averages have been used, and the smoothing rate requires tuning.
Recently, \textcite{karras2023analyzing} proposed new averaging schemes, and importantly a method to perform hyperparameter optimisation of the smoothing rates post training, requiring only a single training run of the model.

\subsubsection{Likelihood training of score-based models}
\label{sec:likelihood_training}
Typically, generative models are trained by minimising the forward Kullback-Leibler divergence between the data distribution and the model.
That is they minimise the following objective \[\c{D}_{KL}\delvert{\pdata}{\pmodelt} &= \int \log \del{\frac{\pdata}{\pmodelt}} \d \pdata \\ &= \int \log \pdata \d \pdata - \int \log \pmodelt \d\pdata \\ &= - \E_{\pdata}\sbr{\log \pmodelt}+ \text{constant} \\& \geq - \E_{\pdata}\sbr{\pmodelt} \quad\quad \text{by Jensen's inequality}.\]
This objective can therefore be seen also as maximising the average log-likelihood of the model under the data, or maximising a lower bound on the likelihood of the model under the data.
It may be that we want to train a score-based model via maximum likelihood.
It is not immediately obvious how to connect score-matching to this objective, however it is possible to do so.


Let us denote the measure placed on the space of diffusion paths defined by the data distribution and the forward noising stochastic differential equation as \(P_\text{data} = \pdata Q^\vsra{}_{\cdot| 0}\) where \(Q^\vsra{}_{\cdot|0}\) is the conditional path measure given an initial data point in the diffusion.
Let us also denote \(Q^\theta = \pprior Q^\vsla{}_\theta(\cdot | T)\) as the measure on paths defined by initialising the parametrised reverse stochastic differential equation with the reference distribution the forward noising process converges to.
Then we can write \[\c{D}_{KL}\delvert{\pdata}{\pmodelt} \leq \c{D}_{KL}\delvert{P_\text{data}}{Q_\theta}\] by the data-processing inequality.
\textcite{huang2021variational,song2021maximum} show that by applying the chain rule for path measures \cite[theorem 2.4]{leonard2014some} and Girsanov's theorem \cite[section 8.6]{oksendal2003stochastic}, for a forward noising process with diffusion term \(\sigma(t)\),
\[\c{D}_{KL}\delvert{P_\text{data}}{Q_\theta} =& \c{D}_{KL}\delvert{p_T}{\pprior} \\&+ \frac{1}{2}\int_0^T \sigma(t)^2 \E_{p_t}\sbr{\norm{\nabla_{\v{X}_t} \log p_t(\v{X}_t) - \v{s}^\theta_t(\v{X}_t)}}_2^2 \d t,\]
showing that optimising any score-matching objective with time weighting \(\lambda(t) = \sigma(t)^2\) maximises the same lower bound on the maximum likelihood of the data as other generative models.

\subsubsection{Likelihood ordinary differential equations}
\label{sec:likelihood-ode}
The final aspect that we are missing is the ability to compute the likelihood of a sample from a diffusion model.
In order to do this we will leverage the \emphmarginnote{Fokker-Plank equation} again (\cref{thm:fokker-plank}).
Recall that for a stochastic differential equation of the form \[\d \v{X}_t = \v{b}(\v{X}_t, t) \d t + \m{\Sigma}(\v{X}_t, t) \d \v{B}_t ,\] the time-evolution of the density is given by
\[ \pd{p_t(\v{X})}{t} = -\sum_{i=1}^d \pd{}{\v{X}_i}\sbr{\v{b}(\v{X}, t) p_t{\v{X}}} + \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^d \sum_{j=1}^d \frac{\partial^2}{\partial \v{X}_i \partial \v{X}_j} \sbr{\m{\Sigma}(\v{X}, t) \m{\Sigma}(\v{X}, t)^T p_t(\v{X})} .\]
Using this, we can obtain the following result:
\begin{theorem}[The likelihood flow ordinary differential equation]
	\label{thm:likelihood_ode}
	For a stochastic differential equation of the form 
	\[\d \v{X}_t = \v{b}(\v{X}_t, t) \d t + \m{\Sigma}(\v{X}_t, t) \d \v{B}_t \]
	initialised with \(\v{X}_0 \sim p_0\), the ordinary differential equation
	\[\d \v{X}_t = \sbr{\v{b}(\v{X}_t, t) - \frac{1}{2}\sbr{\dive \sbr{\m{\Sigma}(\v{X}, t) \m{\Sigma}(\v{X}, t)^T}\nonumber +\m{\Sigma}(\v{X}, t) \m{\Sigma}(\v{X}, t)^T \nabla_{\v{X}_t}\log p_t(\v{X}_t)}} \d t\]
	initialised with \(\v{X}_0 \sim p_0\) has the same marginal density at all times \(t\in\sbr{0,T}\)
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
	Due to \textcite{maoutsa2020interacting} and \textcite[appendix D]{song2020score}.
\end{proof}
In diffusion models we typically use \(\m{\Sigma}(\v{X}, t) = \sigma(t) I\), a simple time-dependant scalar noise.
With this, the likelihood ordinary differential equation simplifies to
\[\d \v{X}_t = \sbr{\v{b}(\v{X}_t, t) - \frac{1}{2}\sigma(t)^2 \nabla_{\v{X}_t}\log p_t(\v{X}_t)} \d t \label{eq:likelihood_ode_drift} .\]
Fortunately there already exist tools to allow us to compute the change in likelihood induced by an ordinary differential equation trajectory, assuming the ordinary differential equation describes the flow of a density.
\begin{theorem}[Instantanious change of likelihood of an ordinary differential equation]
	Let \(\del{\v{X}_t}_{t\in\sbr{0,T}}\) be an ordinary differential equation with governing equation
	\[ \d \v{X}_t = \v{b}(\v{X}_t, t) \d t\]
	and time marginal densities \(p_t(\v{X}_t)\).
	Assume that \(\v{b}\) is uniformly Lipshitz continuous in \(\v{X}_t\) and continuous in \(t\).

	Then, the instantaneous change in log probability is given by 
	\[\pd{\log p_t(\v{X}_t)}{t} = -\tr\del{\eval{\od{\v{b}}{\v{X}_t}}{\del{\v{X}_t, t}}} = -\dive \v{b}\del{\v{X}_t, t}\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
	Proved in \textcite[appendix A]{chen2018neural}.
\end{proof}
Using this, we can evaluate the likelihood of a point under our diffusion model at \(t=0\).
For a given point \(\v{X}_0\) is equal to the change in likelihood over the course of the ordinary differential equation, plus the likelihood of the point at the other end, \[\log p_0(\v{X}_0) = \int_T^0 \dive \v{b}\del{\v{X}_t, t} \d t + \log p_T(\v{X}_T)\]
where \(\v{X}_t\) is the path the point \(\v{X}_0\) follows under the ordinary differential equation and \(p_T\) is the reference distribution of the diffusion model.

In practice, we solve these as a joint ordinary differential equation to take advantage of error tolerant solvers on the joint system,
\[
	\d 
	\begin{bmatrix}
		\v{X}_t \\ \log p(\v{X}_t)
	\end{bmatrix} = 
	\begin{bmatrix}
		\v{b}\del{\v{X}_t, t} \\ \dive \v{b} \del{\v{X}_t, t}
	\end{bmatrix}
\]
This is in fact how \emphmarginnote{continuous normalising flows (CNFs)} \parencite{chen2018neural} work. 
The model is defined by a parametrised drift function \(\v{b}_\theta\), and is trained by maximum likelihood by backpropagating through the ordinary differential equation solver \parencite{chen2018neural}. 
There exist a number of strategies for backpropagating through ordinary differential equation solvers.
These are discussed well in \textcite{kidger2021on}, which has accompanying it the python library \verb|diffrax| \marginnote{\url{https://github.com/patrick-kidger/diffrax}} for handling ordinary differential equations and other differential equations easily.

To apply this scheme for computing the likelihood to diffusion models we simply use our modified drift function in \cref{eq:likelihood_ode_drift} and use the reference distribution from our diffusion model for \(p_T\).

This also gives us an additional method to sample from our diffusion model. 
Instead of solving the stochastic differential equation approximately (\cref{sec:approximate_sde_sampling}) we can sample from \(p_T\) and solve this ordinary differential equation in reverse to draw samples from the model.
This can outperform sampling the stochastic differential equation in certain scenarios \parencite{karras2022Elucidating,song2019generative}.


\subsection{Deep generative modelling on manifolds}

Finally, we discuss prior work on deep generative modelling on manifolds.

We discuss two settings of generative modelling on manifolds.
Namely, the setting of modelling a density on the manifold itself, and also the setting of modelling a density on \emph{signals} on the manifold.
Here we cover methods for which the manifold structure is prescribed, in contrast with methods that jointly learn the manifold structure and density~\parencite[e.g.][]{brehmer2020flows,caterini2021Rectangular}.

\subsubsection{Densities on a manifold}

Simple classical methods exist for fitting mixture of distribution models on manifolds, for example \cite{peel2001fitting} fit mixture of Kent distributions and \textcite{mardia2007protein} fit mixtures of von Mises distributions.
These models struggle with representation power and generalisation in high dimensions.

A series of methods have been proposed that modelled densities on manifolds by defining them as a pushforward of densities modelled on Euclidean space.
For this to work exactly we require a homeomorphism \(\phi: \R^n \to X\).
This can only exist if the manifold is topologically homeomorphic to \(\R^n\), and so is quite limiting.
\textcite{falorsi2019reparameterizing} propose a method of this form for reparametrising densities on Lie groups.
\textcite{bose2020latent} propose a method for modelling densities on hyperbolic space using Euclidean normalising flows pushed onto hyperbolic space.
\textcite{gemici2016normalizing} propose a similar method for generic manifolds.
In addition to the homeomorphism restriction, these methods do not respect the underlying geometry of the space well.
In order to map Euclidean space onto these manifolds, a significant distortion of the metric is required.
This can cause regions of the space that are significantly stretched or squashed by the homeomorphism to be difficult to model accurately.

Methods based on normalising flows have become a popular choice for intrinsically modelling densities on manifolds.
\textcite{rezende2020Normalizing} produce a method for discrete normalising flows that operate on tori and spheres directly.
\textcite{cohen2021riemannian,rezende2021implicit} build normalising flows on manifolds via optimal transport concave potential maps.
Schemes for general equivariant normalising flows on manifolds have also been developed \cite{katsman2021equivariant}.
A significant line of work has been developed for normalising flows on products of the group \(\groupSU[3]\) \cite{abbott2022sampling, abbott2022gauge, abbott2023normalizing}, generic \(\groupSU\) \cite{boyda2021sampling} and \(\groupU[1]\) \cite{kanwar2020equivariant}.
One drawback to normalising flow methods is the difficulty in designing invertible layers for normalising flows.
This makes designing transferable methods for generic manifolds difficult.

Methods based on continuous normalising flows are much easier to design for generic manifolds.
They remove the need to design specific invertible layers to build the flow models, and instead simply require the specification of a time-varying vector field.
Methods extending continuous normalising flows to Riemannian manifolds have been developed by \textcite{falorsi2020neural, falorsi2021Continuous, mathieu2020riemannian, lou2020eural}.
A significant drawback of continuous normalising flows is the need to solve an ordinary differential equation and back-propagate through it at each step of training since this is computationally expensive.
\textcite{rozen2021moser} develop an extension of Riemannian continuous normalising flows by placing a divergence free condition on the vector field. 
This allows them to circumvent the need to compute the ordinary differential equation at the cost of enforcing the divergence free condition via a regulariser.

\subsubsection{Signals on manifolds}

While the literature for modelling densities on Riemannian manifolds is reasonably developed, and there exists significant literature on generative modelling of signals in Euclidean space \cite{goodfellow2014generative,kingma2013auto,rezende2014stochastic,rezende15variational,tabak2010density,tabak2013family,chen2018neural,grathwohl2019scalable,song2020score,sohl2015deep,ho2020denoising} there exists little work on generative modelling of signals on manifolds.

There is a line of work in Gaussian process modelling that can model scalar fields on manifolds \cite{Borovitskiy2020,azangulov2023stationary,azangulov2023stationary2} and vector fields \cite{hutchinson2021vector}.
These are limited however in their expressive power and scalability due to the nature of Gaussian process models.

\textcite{holderith2021equivariant} develop an equivariant neural process model on signals on Euclidean space, and could be extended to modelling signals on manifolds with the kernels defined in \textcite{hutchinson2021vector}.
